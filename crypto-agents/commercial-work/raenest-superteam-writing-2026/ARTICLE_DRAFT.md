# From a Kampala invoice to a digital wallet: making cross-border payments easier to understand

*Original educational writing sample for Raenest x Superteam's 2026 written-content track. Draft, not a published contest entry. The freelancer described below is a hypothetical example, not a claim of personal product usage.*

A freelancer finishes a job for an overseas client. The invoice is agreed, the work is delivered, and the client is ready to pay. But one question can still hold up the whole project: **how will the money arrive, in what currency, and what can the freelancer do with it afterward?**

This is a practical problem for digital workers, small businesses, designers, researchers and remote contractors everywhere. International payment methods do not always line up neatly with the currency a client uses or the local banking methods a worker prefers.

Raenest is a global financial platform built to help individuals and businesses manage money across borders. In addition to multi-currency facilities, it offers dedicated USDC and USDT stablecoin wallets, supported crypto deposits, eligible stablecoin savings features, and cards in supported locations. Rather than promising that every payment is instant or free, the useful question is: **which feature fits the payment that needs to happen?**

## A simple story: the freelancer and the invoice

Imagine a Kampala-based freelance designer working for a company abroad. The invoice is for an agreed USD amount. The company asks whether the designer can receive USDC, a stablecoin intended to track the value of the US dollar.

Before either party transfers anything, they agree on four details: the amount and asset, the blockchain network, the receiving address, and who pays the network fee. These details matter because **USDC on one network is not automatically interchangeable with USDC on another**.

The designer checks whether their Raenest account is eligible for the specific feature and creates the appropriate stablecoin wallet. According to Raenest's published instructions, the user opens **Accounts**, switches to the **Stablecoin** tab, selects USDC or USDT, and follows the wallet-creation steps. The wallet then has a balance, an address and transaction history.

The client can send the agreed stablecoin to the matching address using a network that the wallet supports. Raenest says it supports USDC deposits on several networks, including Solana and Ethereum. A sender must confirm the asset and network against the actual wallet instructions immediately before transferring. A wrong-network transaction may be lost or not credited.

## Why Solana can matter

For a freelancer receiving many small international payments, network cost and settlement time can matter nearly as much as the invoice amount. Solana is designed for relatively low-fee, high-throughput transactions, which can make it useful for compatible stablecoin payments.

That does **not** mean every wallet transaction is instant, free, irreversible or risk-free. Confirmations, platform processing, network congestion and compliance checks can affect the experience. A sensible user checks the applicable fee and actual amount due before paying.

The important comparison is not “crypto versus every bank.” It is whether both sender and receiver can use the *same supported payment rail* with clear terms.

## What happens after USDC or USDT arrives?

Receiving a payment is only the first part of the job. The freelancer may need to hold the funds temporarily, convert part into another supported currency, or spend from an available balance.

Raenest describes separate USDC and USDT wallets. Incoming supported stablecoin deposits are credited to the corresponding wallet, rather than automatically being converted into ordinary USD. Where the relevant service is available, a customer can review their balance and decide whether to hold, convert, send or use eligible spending options.

That distinction matters. “A dollar-equivalent stablecoin” is not the same thing as “cash already deposited in my local bank.” Conversions, rates, limits and service availability should be checked before making a budget or promising a supplier a settlement date.

For example, a freelancer might separate money reserved for internet access, software subscriptions and project expenses from the amount available to spend. The platform can support a workflow, but the freelancer still needs to keep accurate records of invoices, fees, transfers and actual balances.

## Can someone deposit BTC, ETH or SOL?

Raenest also describes a route for depositing BTC, ETH and SOL that converts eligible deposits into USDC. This is different from receiving USDC directly into a USDC wallet. The exact destination, required confirmations, expected conversion and fees must be checked inside the app for the specific transaction.

Do not assume that a Bitcoin address can receive Solana tokens or that a stablecoin address can receive native BTC. Every crypto deposit needs a matching asset and network.

A practical demonstration could show the *on-screen steps* for a supported SOL deposit, with private details obscured, but it should only be described as a completed transaction if someone genuinely made it. No transaction is claimed in this writing sample.

## Saving: what “up to 7% APY” actually means

Raenest advertises an optional Stablecoin Vault for eligible USDC and USDT balances, with an **up to 7% variable annual percentage yield (APY)** and a stated minimum of $100. The “up to” and “variable” parts matter: they are not a fixed guaranteed return, and the feature does not apply simply because someone holds BTC, ETH or SOL.

An annual percentage yield is not a promise that 7% will be earned in a week or month. Users should review eligibility, withdrawal rules, rates, underlying risk and product terms before deciding whether the feature fits their situation.

For a working freelancer, a Vault may be something to evaluate only after separating short-term business expenses from funds genuinely available to save. Money needed to pay a bill tomorrow should not be treated casually as long-term savings.

## Spending: virtual and physical cards

Raenest also offers virtual and physical card products for eligible countries and accounts. In Uganda, the company's help pages list support for virtual cards, but account approval, card availability, usage limits and fees still depend on the actual user and current terms.

A card can be useful when an online service accepts the supported card network. It is not a guarantee that every merchant, country, transaction or crypto balance will work. Review any charges and which balance will actually be debited.

## A five-point payment checklist

Before a real payment, the freelancer and client should confirm:

1. **The commercial agreement:** what work was accepted and how much is due.
2. **The correct asset:** USDC, USDT, BTC, ETH or SOL, with no ambiguous label such as “crypto.”
3. **The exact network and receiving address:** confirmed in the actual wallet, not copied from an old message.
4. **The expected deductions and timing:** network fees, service charges, conversion rates and confirmations.
5. **The evidence:** invoice reference, transaction identifier and account credit, reconciled to the actual amount received.

This checklist does more for a trustworthy payment experience than a dramatic promise about getting rich quickly. It lets a client pay correctly, lets the freelancer verify the receipt, and helps both parties explain what happened.

## The real takeaway

A borderless payment product becomes useful when it solves a specific everyday problem: a completed invoice needs to turn into money a person can verify, manage and use. Stablecoins, supported crypto deposits, multi-currency balances, eligible Vault savings and card access can be different parts of that journey.
