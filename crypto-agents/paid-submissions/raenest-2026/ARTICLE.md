## The invoice is paid. Why can’t I use the money yet?

Imagine Asha, a freelance designer working with a client across borders. She invoices for a finished project, but their payment method and her day-to-day spending needs don't line up. The client can pay USDC; Asha wants a reliable way to receive it, keep a clear record, decide what to hold and what to spend, and avoid moving funds through unnecessary intermediaries.

This is a *hypothetical scenario*, not a claim that Asha used Raenest. It illustrates a real challenge: the moment someone says “paid in crypto,” the work is only half done. The other half is routing the payment correctly, confirming that it arrived, tracking its value and making it usable.

Raenest positions itself as one app for receiving, managing, converting, saving and spending money across borders. In its current materials, it describes dedicated wallets for USDC and USDT, selected cryptocurrency deposit routes, a Stablecoin Vault for eligible users, and card spending where available.

## Step 1: Agree on **both** the coin and the network

Asha and her client should agree in writing on the exact settlement asset and network, not just “send crypto.” For example, USDC on Solana is not the same transaction route as USDC on Ethereum or Base. Using an unsupported coin/network combination can put a payment at risk.

Raenest's support materials list USDC deposits across selected networks, including Solana; USDT support differs by network. The *in-app deposit screen* is the source of truth for the recipient's particular account. Asha would open her USDC wallet, choose the supported network, copy the generated deposit address, and independently confirm that the client has selected the same chain. A small test transfer may be prudent for a first-time relationship, allowing for any costs.

A professional payment instruction also names the invoice, amount, token, selected network, and how both parties will identify the final transaction. That is not extra paperwork: it prevents “we sent it” from becoming a week of reconciliation.

## Step 2: Confirm the actual receipt

After the client transmits the payment, Asha should verify the credited balance and the incoming transaction history rather than rely on a screenshot from the sender. The sender's transaction hash can help reconcile payment to invoice, but a transaction broadcast is not the same as a confirmed credit.

Raenest says USDC and USDT deposits appear in dedicated matching stablecoin wallets, rather than being automatically converted to traditional currency. That separation matters for record keeping: receipt, conversion and spending can be distinct events, each with its own timing, rate, fee and supporting evidence.

For a freelancer or small business, a simple ledger can capture:

| Record | Example field |
| --- | --- |
| Invoice reference | INV-024 |
| Contract settlement asset | USDC |
| Agreed network | Solana (only if supported in recipient's app) |
| Gross amount requested | 500 USDC |
| Transaction reference | Verified transaction hash |
| Net amount credited | Actual wallet entry, not an estimate |
| Later conversion/spend | Date, amount and applicable fee |

## Step 3: Choose deliberately between holding, converting and spending

Receiving USDC is a payment milestone, not a financial plan. Asha might need some of the proceeds for rent and internet, some for software, and some as a reserve for an upcoming project. Different funds have different jobs.

Raenest's published guides describe the ability to hold USDC/USDT, convert into supported currencies, send across supported networks, and spend from stablecoin balances using a Raenest card wherever the card and merchant are supported. The app should show fees and exchange quotes before confirmation. Availability can vary by account and jurisdiction.

The useful question is not “Which coin will increase in price?” but “How much of my balance must remain available to meet obligations already due?” That keeps a freelancer focused on cash flow, not speculation.

Asha should retain the invoice, the received amount, conversion confirmations and card-transaction records. If her client paid for deliverables in USDC, she still needs to record the appropriate value and relevant tax information under her own country's rules.

## Step 4: Understand the Vault—and its limitations

Raenest's Stablecoin Vault is designed for **eligible USDC and USDT balances**. Its published offer says *up to 7% variable APY*, with a $100 minimum deposit, daily accrual/compounding after the initial period, and withdrawals subject to the applicable terms and fees.

An APY is an annualized yield figure, **not** a promise that a given balance will earn 7% every year. Rates may change. Returns are not the same as a bank-deposit guarantee; stablecoins, custodial arrangements, access restrictions and platform operations carry risks. No one should deposit an emergency fund they cannot afford to have temporarily unavailable merely because the word “stable” appears in the asset name.

Asha's first task would be to read the current Vault terms, check her eligibility, review the live displayed yield and withdrawal fees, and decide whether any balance is truly surplus to near-term needs. BTC, ETH and SOL do **not** earn the advertised Stablecoin Vault APY simply by being held as those assets.

## Step 5: The extra route—funding with SOL, BTC or ETH

Raenest's November 2026 guide says users may fund their account by sending BTC, ETH or SOL to a *specially generated deposit route* and receiving the converted value in their USDC wallet. The critical detail is that a crypto funding route should never be confused with a direct like-for-like token wallet.

If Asha were paid in SOL instead of USDC, her checklist would be: select SOL in the app's **Add Money** flow, verify the current supported address/network, review conversion and fees, perform the transfer, and verify the USDC credit. She should not assume the credited asset remains SOL or send tokens to an address intended for a different coin.

## A better checklist for the next international invoice

Before the client pays: agree on the currency, network, required invoice details, and who bears transfer fees.

When the client pays: verify the correct recipient deposit screen, match the network, and preserve the transaction reference.

When the money lands: confirm the actual credited amount, reconcile the invoice, and keep each transfer, conversion and spending entry distinct.

Before earning yield: check local eligibility, minimum amount, rate variability, custody risks and withdrawal charges.

Before spending: check card availability, available balance, current quote, fees and local rules.

The broader lesson is that borderless money needs borderless *discipline*. Stablecoins can simplify the movement of value, but the strongest workflow is the one a freelancer or small business can explain, reconcile and repeat.
